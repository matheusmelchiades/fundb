# AI Regulations & Auditability Requirements

**Date:** 2026-03-01
**Researcher:** AI Agent
**Method:** Regulatory document analysis + secondary research

## Executive Summary

AI regulations are creating genuine demand for auditability, traceability, and data provenance -- but the demand concentrates at the **model lifecycle and MLOps layer**, not at the database layer. The EU AI Act (effective August 2026 for high-risk systems), FDA AI/ML guidelines, and financial regulators (SR 11-7, SS1/23) all mandate audit trails, logging, explainability, and data governance. However, the compliance tooling market has responded primarily through **MLOps platforms** (MLflow, Weights & Biases, Databricks), **AI governance tools** (Credo AI, OneTrust, Collibra), and **model monitoring tools** (Arize, WhyLabs) -- not specialized databases.

A database with native confidence, provenance, and bitemporal tracking would address a **real but narrow** subset of compliance requirements -- primarily around data lineage, temporal auditability ("what did the system believe at time T?"), and contradiction detection. The strongest fit is in healthcare (FDA) and financial services (SR 11-7, SS1/23) where data provenance requirements are most granular. However, teams today solve these problems with combinations of existing tools, not purpose-built databases. The question is whether a database-native approach is sufficiently better to displace entrenched toolchains.

**Bottom line:** Regulations create real demand for the *capabilities* FunDB offers, but not necessarily for a *new database* to deliver them. The competitive moat depends on whether database-level integration is meaningfully superior to the MLOps-plus-governance-tool stack that enterprises already use.

## EU AI Act

### Key Requirements

The EU AI Act is the world's first comprehensive AI regulation. The remaining provisions become applicable on **August 2, 2026** for high-risk AI systems.

- **Article 9 -- Risk Management System**: High-risk AI systems must have a risk management system that runs throughout the entire lifecycle of the system, including identification and analysis of known and reasonably foreseeable risks, estimation and evaluation of risks, and adoption of appropriate risk management measures.

- **Article 10 -- Data Governance**: Training, validation, and testing datasets must be relevant, sufficiently representative, and free of errors. Providers must document data sources, provenance, scope, and main characteristics. This includes how data was obtained, selected, and labeled.

- **Article 11 -- Technical Documentation**: Must be drawn up before placing the system on the market and must demonstrate compliance with all requirements. Annex IV specifies documentation of training data provenance, design specifications, and validation methodologies.

- **Article 12 -- Record-Keeping (Logging)**: High-risk AI systems must technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over their lifetime. Logs must enable: (a) identifying risk situations, (b) facilitating post-market monitoring, and (c) monitoring operation. Logs must be kept for **at least 6 months**.

- **Article 13 -- Transparency**: Systems must be designed to be sufficiently transparent for deployers to understand functioning and output. Instructions for use must include information about capabilities, limitations, and risks.

- **Article 14 -- Human Oversight**: High-risk AI systems must be designed to allow effective human oversight, including the ability to correctly interpret outputs, decide not to use the system, and intervene or interrupt.

- **Article 19 -- Automatically Generated Logs**: Deployers must keep logs automatically generated by high-risk AI systems, to the extent such logs are under their control, for a minimum of **6 months**.

- **Annex IV -- Technical Documentation**: Requires documentation of training data sets including provenance, scope, main characteristics, how data was obtained and selected, labeling procedures, data cleaning, and data preparation methodologies.

### Mapping to Database Capabilities

| Requirement | Relevant FunDB Capability | Gap/Fit Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Article 10 -- Data provenance documentation | `_sources` with origin, method, confidence | **Partial fit.** FunDB tracks provenance per-record, but EU AI Act requires provenance of *training datasets*, not inference-time data. Training data provenance is typically handled by data versioning tools (DVC, lakeFS). |
| Article 12 -- Automatic event logging | Audit logging, bitemporal MVCC | **Good fit.** Bitemporal tracking naturally answers "what was the system state at time T?" -- directly relevant to post-incident investigation. However, application-level logging tools (ELK stack, Datadog) also serve this purpose. |
| Article 13 -- Transparency/explainability | Causal reasoning, confidence scoring | **Weak fit.** Explainability requirements focus on *model* transparency (why did the model produce this output?), not *data* transparency. Confidence scores on data records are adjacent but not what regulators are asking for. |
| Article 14 -- Human oversight | Contradiction detection | **Marginal fit.** Contradiction detection could flag inconsistent outputs for human review, but human oversight requirements are primarily about UI/UX design and process controls, not database features. |
| Annex IV -- Training data documentation | Source provenance tracking | **Partial fit.** Relevant for documenting data lineage, but training data documentation is a one-time compliance activity, not an ongoing database function. |
| 10-year documentation retention | Bitemporal MVCC | **Moderate fit.** Bitemporal storage naturally supports long-term retention with full historical queryability. This is a genuine advantage over append-only logs. |

### Timeline & Enforcement

- **February 2025**: Prohibitions on unacceptable-risk AI practices took effect.
- **August 2025**: Obligations for general-purpose AI models applied.
- **August 2, 2026**: Full requirements for high-risk AI systems become applicable.
- **Penalties**: Up to 35 million EUR or 7% of global annual turnover for the most serious violations.

## FDA AI/ML Guidelines

### Key Requirements

The FDA has issued several guidance documents governing AI/ML in medical devices (Software as a Medical Device, SaMD):

- **Total Product Lifecycle (TPLC) Approach** (Draft Guidance, January 2025): Recommends submissions include model description, **data lineage/splits**, performance tied to claims, bias analysis/mitigation, human-AI workflow, monitoring, and a Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP).

- **Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP)** (Final Guidance, December 2024): Allows manufacturers to pre-authorize certain AI model modifications without new marketing submissions. Requires documentation of:
  - Data management practices (representative training/tuning/test data, multisite/sequestered test sets, bias-mitigation strategies)
  - Retraining practices (what parts of the model may change, triggers, overfitting controls)
  - Performance evaluation (study designs, metrics, acceptance criteria, statistical plans)
  - Update procedures (deployment mechanics, user communication, labeling updates, cybersecurity validation, real-world monitoring, **rollback criteria**)

- **Good Machine Learning Practice (GMLP)** (2021 Guiding Principles): 10 principles including data quality assurance, independence of training/tuning/test datasets, model transparency, and real-world performance monitoring.

- **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)**: FDA requires tracking of all software components and their vulnerabilities.

- **Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR)**: Effective February 2, 2026, aligning FDA Part 820 with ISO 13485 -- requires design history files and traceability.

### Mapping to Database Capabilities

| Requirement | Relevant FunDB Capability | Gap/Fit Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Data lineage/splits documentation | `_sources` provenance tracking | **Moderate fit.** Tracking data lineage from collection through transformation is valuable. But FDA data lineage focuses on training data pipelines, typically managed by DVC/lakeFS/MLflow, not the inference database. |
| PCCP rollback criteria | Bitemporal MVCC | **Good fit.** Bitemporal tracking directly supports "roll back to the state of the data as it existed at time T" -- valuable for PCCP compliance where you must demonstrate what data was used for each model version. |
| Real-world performance monitoring | Confidence scoring, audit logging | **Moderate fit.** Confidence drift on incoming data could signal distribution shift. But performance monitoring is typically done by dedicated tools (Arize, WhyLabs, EvidentlyAI). |
| Bias analysis/mitigation | Causal reasoning | **Interesting but unproven.** Causal reasoning could theoretically help identify confounders in bias analysis. But this is a research-grade capability, not a compliance checkbox. |
| SBOM and component tracking | Not directly relevant | **No fit.** SBOM is about software dependencies, not data. |

### Timeline

- PCCP Final Guidance: December 2024 (in effect).
- TPLC Draft Guidance: January 2025 (comment period closed April 2025; expected finalization late 2025/early 2026).
- QMSR alignment with ISO 13485: Effective February 2, 2026.
- FY-2026 Guidance Agenda: Additional lifecycle management guidance expected.

## Financial Regulations (SR 11-7, SS1/23, SEC)

### SR 11-7 (US Federal Reserve -- Model Risk Management)

**Issued:** April 4, 2011 (still the governing standard)

Key requirements:
- **Rigorous assessment of data quality and relevance**: All models must be built on sound data with documented quality assessments.
- **Model validation**: Independent validation of model methodology, data inputs, outputs, and assumptions.
- **Documentation**: Sufficiently detailed that parties unfamiliar with a model can understand how it operates, its limitations, and key assumptions.
- **Outcomes analysis**: Comparison of model outputs with corresponding actual outcomes (backtesting).
- **Model inventory**: Comprehensive inventory of all models in use.
- **Ongoing monitoring**: Tracking model performance over time, including stability of inputs and parameters.

### SS1/23 (Bank of England/PRA -- Model Risk Management)

**Effective:** May 17, 2024

Five core principles:
1. **Model identification and risk classification**: Firms must maintain a comprehensive inventory and classify models by risk tier.
2. **Governance**: Board-level accountability for model risk; clear roles and responsibilities.
3. **Model development, implementation and use**: Sound development practices including data quality standards, documentation, and testing. Explicitly covers AI/ML as a sub-principle.
4. **Independent model validation**: Separate validation function with authority to challenge.
5. **Model risk mitigants**: Controls to reduce residual model risk, including limits, overrides, and fallback procedures.

### SEC AI Disclosure

**Status:** Nascent and evolving

- December 2025: SEC Investor Advisory Committee recommended disclosure guidelines for AI, but the SEC has not yet adopted formal rules.
- The SEC withdrew Biden-era proposed rules on AI conflicts of interest (June 2025).
- 2026 exam priorities focus on AI use, training, and security controls.
- The approach remains **principles-based**, relying on existing materiality-based disclosure frameworks rather than prescriptive AI-specific rules.

### Mapping to Database Capabilities

| Requirement | Relevant FunDB Capability | Gap/Fit Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| SR 11-7: Data quality assessment | Confidence scoring, provenance tracking | **Moderate fit.** Confidence scores per-record could support data quality documentation. But model risk teams use statistical tools (SAS, R, Python) for data quality assessment, not database-native features. |
| SR 11-7: Outcomes analysis (backtesting) | Bitemporal MVCC | **Good fit.** "What did the model see at time T, and what happened after?" is exactly what bitemporal queries answer. This is one of the strongest compliance use cases. |
| SR 11-7: Ongoing monitoring | Confidence scoring, contradiction detection | **Moderate fit.** Detecting confidence drift or contradictions in data could support monitoring. But dedicated model monitoring tools (Arize, EvidentlyAI) are the standard approach. |
| SS1/23: Model inventory and classification | Not directly relevant | **No fit.** Model inventory is a governance function, not a database function. |
| SS1/23: AI/ML-specific governance | Audit logging, provenance | **Weak fit.** The regulation addresses organizational governance (roles, policies, processes), not data infrastructure. |
| SEC: Materiality-based disclosure | Not directly relevant | **No fit.** Disclosure is a reporting and legal function. |

## NIST AI RMF & ISO 42001

### NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)

**Status:** Voluntary framework (published January 2023)

Key aspects relevant to data infrastructure:
- **GOVERN function**: Establish policies, processes, and structures for AI risk management, including internal standards for data provenance, model explainability, and fairness thresholds.
- **MAP function**: Identify and document AI system context, including data sources and their provenance.
- **MEASURE function**: Quantify and track identified risks, including data quality metrics.
- **MANAGE function**: Allocate resources and implement plans to address mapped and measured risks.

NIST AI 600-1 (Generative AI supplement) specifically addresses:
- **Content provenance**: Recording and revealing the provenance of content, including source and history of changes.
- **Data provenance**: Tracking information about origins and history of digital content to assist in authenticity determinations.
- **Supply chain diligence**: Including data provenance and integrity checks.

### ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (AI Management System)

**Status:** Published December 2023; certifiable standard

Key requirements:
- **AI Policy**: Comprehensive organizational policy for AI development and use.
- **Risk assessment**: AI system impact assessment including data-related risks.
- **System lifecycle management**: Controls across the full AI system lifecycle.
- **Third-party supplier oversight**: Including data suppliers.
- **Performance evaluation**: Monitoring, measurement, analysis, and evaluation of AI systems.
- **38 distinct controls**: Covering governance, risk, development, deployment, and monitoring.

### Mapping to Database Capabilities

| Requirement | Relevant FunDB Capability | Gap/Fit Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| NIST: Data provenance tracking | `_sources` provenance tracking | **Moderate fit.** NIST recommends provenance tracking, but primarily for training data and content authentication, not inference-time data stores. |
| NIST: Content provenance (GenAI) | Source provenance with origin/method | **Interesting fit.** For RAG systems, tracking which sources contributed to a generated answer maps well to FunDB's `_sources`. This is one of the more compelling use cases. |
| ISO 42001: System lifecycle management | Bitemporal MVCC, audit logging | **Partial fit.** Lifecycle management is primarily an organizational process, but temporal data management supports the technical implementation. |
| ISO 42001: Performance evaluation | Confidence scoring | **Weak fit.** Performance evaluation refers to model performance metrics, not data-level confidence. |

## Synthesis: Where Does Database-Level Tracking Actually Help?

### Where Database-Level Tracking Adds Genuine Value

1. **Bitemporal queries for backtesting and incident investigation**: The ability to answer "what did the system know/believe at time T?" is genuinely valuable for SR 11-7 outcomes analysis, EU AI Act post-incident investigation (Article 12), and FDA PCCP rollback requirements. MLOps tools version models but rarely version the underlying data with full temporal queryability. **This is the strongest compliance argument for FunDB.**

2. **RAG system provenance**: For retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems, tracking which knowledge-base documents contributed to an answer -- with confidence scores -- maps well to EU AI Act transparency requirements and NIST content provenance recommendations. This is an emerging use case that existing tools do not serve well.

3. **Data quality signals**: Confidence scoring per-record could support SR 11-7 data quality documentation and EU AI Act data governance requirements. However, this is supplementary to, not a replacement for, statistical data quality tools.

### Where Database-Level Tracking Is Insufficient or Redundant

1. **Model explainability**: All major regulations require explanations of *model* behavior -- why did the model make this decision? This is fundamentally about model architecture and interpretation (SHAP, LIME, attention weights), not about the data layer. FunDB's causal reasoning is intellectually interesting but does not address the specific explainability requirements regulators enforce.

2. **Training data provenance**: The EU AI Act (Annex IV) and FDA (TPLC) require documentation of training data pipelines. This is handled by data versioning tools (DVC, lakeFS, Delta Lake) and experiment tracking tools (MLflow, W&B), which are designed for batch data pipeline management -- a fundamentally different workload than a query database.

3. **Model lifecycle governance**: SS1/23, SR 11-7, and ISO 42001 all require organizational governance structures (model inventories, validation functions, approval workflows). These are workflow and governance tools (Credo AI, OneTrust, ModelOp), not database features.

4. **Audit logging**: While FunDB's audit logging is relevant, application-level and infrastructure-level logging (ELK, Splunk, Datadog) are the entrenched solutions. Adding audit logging at the database layer does not displace these tools.

### What Teams Actually Use Today

Based on research, the typical enterprise AI compliance stack is:

| Layer | Common Tools | FunDB Overlap |
|---|---|---|
| **Experiment tracking** | MLflow, W&B, Neptune | Low |
| **Data versioning** | DVC, lakeFS, Delta Lake | Low-Medium |
| **Model registry** | MLflow, SageMaker, Vertex AI | None |
| **Model monitoring** | Arize, WhyLabs, EvidentlyAI | Low |
| **AI governance** | Credo AI, OneTrust, Collibra, ModelOp | Low |
| **Audit logging** | Splunk, ELK, Datadog | Low-Medium |
| **Data quality** | Great Expectations, Soda, dbt tests | Low |
| **Knowledge management (RAG)** | Vector DBs + custom code | **Medium-High** |

The RAG knowledge management layer is the most underserved by existing tools and where FunDB's provenance + confidence + bitemporal combination could be most differentiated.

## Signals for/against Hypothesis H5

*H5: AI regulations requiring auditability create demand for database-level tracking of confidence, provenance, and causality.*

### Evidence FOR

- The EU AI Act (Articles 10, 12, 19) creates **legally binding** requirements for data provenance documentation, automatic event logging, and record retention -- applicable to all high-risk AI systems by August 2026, with penalties up to 35M EUR / 7% of global turnover.
- FDA PCCP requires detailed data management practices and rollback capabilities -- bitemporal data is a natural fit.
- SR 11-7 backtesting requirements ("what did the model believe at time T?") map directly to bitemporal MVCC.
- NIST AI 600-1 specifically identifies data provenance for generative AI as a key risk management concern.
- The Fortune 500 Data & Trust Alliance has published proposed data provenance standards, indicating enterprise recognition of the need.
- Financial services regulations (SS1/23 effective 2024, SR 11-7 ongoing) create compliance budgets that could fund new tooling.
- RAG systems are proliferating rapidly and have no standard provenance tracking solution.

### Evidence AGAINST

- **Regulations address model-level concerns, not data-level concerns.** The bulk of compliance effort goes into model validation, governance processes, and organizational controls -- not data infrastructure.
- **Entrenched toolchains exist.** MLflow, W&B, Credo AI, Arize, and dozens of other tools already serve AI compliance. Enterprises have invested in these stacks and have no urgent reason to add or replace a database layer.
- **Training data provenance != inference data provenance.** The most specific data provenance requirements (EU AI Act Annex IV, FDA TPLC) target training pipelines, which are batch workloads served by data versioning tools, not query databases.
- **Compliance buyers are risk-averse.** Regulated enterprises (banks, pharma, medical device companies) are among the most conservative technology adopters. A startup database with no compliance certification history would face enormous credibility barriers.
- **"Good enough" solutions exist.** Teams solve audit trail requirements with Splunk + MLflow + spreadsheets. It is not elegant, but it works, and it does not require a new database.
- **Voluntary frameworks (NIST, ISO 42001) do not create purchasing urgency.** Only binding regulations with penalties (EU AI Act, FDA, SR 11-7) drive real compliance spending.
- **SEC has pulled back**, withdrawing Biden-era AI rules and not yet adopting the Investor Advisory Committee's recommendations.

### Strength of Signal: 2.5 out of 5

Regulations create demand for the *capabilities* but not clearly for a *new database product*. The signal is mixed -- there is a real compliance need, but it is being met by other tools, and the specific database-layer advantages (bitemporal queries, per-record confidence) are niche within the broader compliance landscape.

## Skeptical Assessment

### Is compliance-driven demand for a specialized database real?

**Partially.** The demand for auditability and provenance is unquestionably real and growing. The EU AI Act alone will affect thousands of companies by August 2026. However, the demand is for *compliance outcomes* (audit-ready documentation, explainable decisions, traceable data), not for specific infrastructure products. Teams assemble compliance from whatever tools they already have.

### Would teams buy a new database for compliance?

**Unlikely as a standalone purchase.** Regulated enterprises do not typically adopt new databases to solve compliance problems -- they add governance layers on top of existing infrastructure. The more realistic path would be:

1. Teams adopt FunDB for its **primary value proposition** (e.g., RAG knowledge management with confidence and provenance), and
2. Compliance capabilities become a **secondary selling point** that helps justify the purchase to risk/compliance teams.

This is the classic enterprise pattern: the technical team buys for functionality; the business case includes compliance as a "bonus."

### Would a DB startup have credibility with regulated enterprises?

**Not initially.** Regulated enterprises in finance and healthcare require:
- SOC 2 Type II certification
- Proven production reliability at scale
- Reference customers in their specific industry
- Endorsement or recognition by relevant regulators or standards bodies

A startup database would need 2-3 years of production track record before regulated enterprises would consider it for compliance-critical workloads. The credibility path would be: (1) gain adoption in non-regulated or less-regulated environments, (2) accumulate production evidence, (3) obtain certifications, (4) then target regulated industries.

### The honest comparison

| Compliance Task | FunDB Approach | Current Industry Approach | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| "What data was used at time T?" | Bitemporal MVCC query | Data snapshots in S3 + MLflow run metadata | FunDB (cleaner) |
| "Where did this data come from?" | `_sources` metadata per record | DVC + lakeFS lineage tracking | Tie |
| "How confident is this data?" | Native confidence field | Custom metadata or quality scores | FunDB (if adopted) |
| "Why did the model decide X?" | Causal reasoning | SHAP/LIME on model layer | Industry tools |
| "Show me the audit trail" | Built-in audit log | Splunk/ELK + application logs | Industry tools |
| "Prove compliance to regulators" | No certification | Credo AI/OneTrust with certifications | Industry tools |

FunDB wins narrowly on temporal data queries and loses on the broader compliance ecosystem. The most defensible positioning is not "compliance database" but rather "AI knowledge infrastructure that happens to be compliance-friendly."
